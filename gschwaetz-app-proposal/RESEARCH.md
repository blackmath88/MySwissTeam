# Research foundation

This document translates relevant research traditions into product design principles. It is not a claim that any single study directly validates the entire Gschwätz interaction model.

## 1. Affective polarisation

Political conflict is not only about policy disagreement. It can become social: in-group attachment and out-group dislike shape interpretation before arguments are evaluated.

### Design implication

Delay labels and identity cues during first contact with a perspective.

**Design pattern:**

`perspective first -> identity later`

The goal is not to hide identity permanently, but to alter the order of exposure.

## 2. Motivated reasoning and identity-protective cognition

People are often more critical of information that threatens prior commitments and more accepting of information that supports them.

### Design implication

Avoid opening with a demand for commitment such as "Are you for or against?"

Instead begin with curiosity:

- What matters to this person?
- What problem do they think is real?
- What do they fear losing?
- What is their strongest evidence?

## 3. Psychological reactance

People resist messages that feel coercive or autonomy-threatening.

### Design implication

The system should offer conversational agency.

Prefer:

- "Want to hear the strongest objection?"
- "Should I challenge this view or explain it further?"
- "Which part do you want to examine?"

Avoid:

- "You should reconsider."
- "You need to understand that..."
- "Experts prove that your view is wrong."

## 4. Intellectual humility

Political conversations become more productive when people can recognise limits to their own knowledge and uncertainty.

### Design implication

Avatars should not sound omniscient.

Each perspective must contain:

- uncertainty
- unresolved trade-offs
- strongest counterargument
- conditions that could change the position

Example:

> "I still lean yes, but the implementation risk is the strongest reason I hesitate."

## 5. Steelmanning

A useful deliberative norm is to present opposing arguments in forms their proponents would recognise as fair.

### Design implication

Each avatar must be able to answer:

> "What is the strongest reasonable argument against your own position?"

Evaluation should fail personas that strawman opponents.

## 6. Moral reframing

Political positions are often grounded in different value vocabularies. Communication can become more intelligible when arguments are translated across value frames.

### Design implication

Represent values explicitly rather than only positions.

Useful value dimensions:

- fairness
- autonomy
- security
- responsibility
- solidarity
- institutional trust
- efficiency
- sustainability
- equality
- order

This should support understanding, not covert persuasion.

## 7. Deliberation and open communication

Good political discussion is not necessarily adversarial debate. Collaborative or open conversational formats can support reason-giving, listening, and recognition of disagreement without requiring consensus.

### Design implication

The app should not be framed as "beat the other side" or "win the argument".

Conversation modes:

- Understand
- Challenge
- Find common ground
- Compare trade-offs
- Ask for evidence

## 8. Curiosity and information seeking

Curiosity can help people tolerate uncertainty and explore unfamiliar viewpoints.

### Design implication

Reward discovery rather than correctness.

Examples:

- "You found a tension."
- "You discovered an argument you did not expect."
- "This question revealed a trade-off."

Avoid points for "correct political answers".

## 9. Narrative transportation and character-based reasoning

People engage differently with positions when they are embedded in a human-scale perspective rather than presented as abstract bullet points.

### Design implication

Give arguments a situated voice, but keep fact / position / lived frame distinct.

Three-layer model:

1. **FACT** — what is known or claimed in source material
2. **POSITION** — what conclusion the avatar draws
3. **LIVED FRAME** — why this matters from this perspective

Do not invent biographical trauma, identity, or personal experience merely for emotional effect.

## 10. Prediction error / expectation violation

Attention increases when a person encounters something that violates a stereotype or expected category mapping.

### Design implication

Avatars should be coherent but not stereotyped.

A pro-business argument may contain a social fairness concern. A left-leaning perspective may acknowledge regulatory limits. A conservative argument may strongly value environmental stewardship.

The reveal works because the user discovers that political positions are not perfectly reducible to labels.

## 11. Contact and perspective-taking

Perspective-taking can improve understanding, but poorly designed forced empathy can backfire.

### Design implication

Do not tell users to "feel empathy" or morally reward one perspective.

Invite voluntary exploration:

> "Pick the person you understand least."

## Research-to-product summary

| Research idea | Product mechanic |
|---|---|
| Affective polarisation | Delay partisan identity |
| Motivated reasoning | Avoid early stance declaration |
| Reactance | User-controlled conversation modes |
| Intellectual humility | Avatars express uncertainty |
| Steelmanning | Strongest opposing argument required |
| Moral reframing | Values visible behind reasons |
| Deliberation | Open conversation, no winner |
| Curiosity | Reward discovery, not correctness |
| Narrative | Human-scale argument personas |
| Expectation violation | Non-stereotypical coherent personas |
| Provenance / trust | Reveal sources after conversation |
